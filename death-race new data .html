Project snapshot

Death Race is a combat-racing game with rockets, nitro, shields and health pickups. It lived inside an existing ready-to-play platform that hosted many other games.

I worked across game design, art coordination, audio, VFX and implementation, partnering with the wider team to deliver the experience.

What I did, at a glance

  • Designed the lock-and-go path from entry screen to race, and the staged in-race tutorial.
  • Planned track sections: obstacles, pickup routes and likely battle spots.
  • Balanced the racing AI and designed a way to classify players and adapt difficulty.
  • Iterated through user tests for racing veterans and for newcomers.
  • Designed and shipped a conditional repositioning solution for AI opponents that drifted out of the race.
  • Created the sound design and supervised VFX implementation across the game.
  • Coordinated with the art team on car colours and look.

The design challenge

Three pressures shaped almost every decision I made.

Seconds, not screens

The entry flow needed to get players to the first race without unnecessary onboarding or navigation.

One race, two kinds of player

Racing veterans want opponents that push back. People new to racing games want to feel they are getting somewhere. One AI setting cannot serve both.

AI the engine would not keep honest

Engine and implementation constraints could cause opponents to drift away from the active race. I addressed this through conditional AI repositioning.

The opponent should feel present, never absent, and never all-knowing.A guiding principle for AI balancing and opponent repositioning.

Why the tutorial lives inside the race

A tutorial screen asks players to wait before they have done anything. Here the car accelerates by itself, so players are racing within seconds. Each control is introduced only when it becomes useful.

  • One UI button is introduced at a time.
  • Steering teaches left and right together.
  • Players start with one rocket and one boost, so the first use is guaranteed.

The staged prompt sequence is delivered in the first-race flow. Each prompt introduces a useful action when the player can apply it.

Player testing and iteration

This was the engine of the balance work. I watched people play, wrote down where they struggled, changed something and watched again. I did it for two audiences.

  1. Play

    Observe players in a build.

  2. Capture

    Record behaviour and what they say.

  3. Diagnose

    Find the friction or the difficulty mismatch.

  4. Tune

    Change a parameter, layout or prompt.

  5. Re-test

    Play again and compare.

Repeat with both audiences

Experienced racing players

They know the genre. They want a contest.

Balancing focus

  • Keep opponents active and competitive rather than easy to ignore.
  • Make pickup and ability use create meaningful counterplay.
  • Keep catch-up pressure exciting without making it feel unfair.

Levers in the datasheet

TargetMinSpeed, ShootingProbability, ShieldingProbability, MaxThrottle

Players new to racing games

They are still learning controls and genre habits.

Balancing focus

  • Make controls and first-race prompts easy to understand.
  • Give players room to recover instead of overwhelming them immediately.
  • Make rockets and nitro readable, learnable parts of the race.

Levers in the datasheet

Noob column, ShootingToAlProbability, MaxThrottle (1.5 for Noob), FTUE prompt timing

Feedback from both audiences informed changes to AI speed, ability-use probabilities and onboarding. Easy, New Easy and Noob profiles support differentiated tuning rather than a single difficulty curve.

Technical constraint and design response

AI opponents could drift away from the player because of engine and implementation constraints. I designed and shipped conditional repositioning to bring opponents back into a relevant race position.

Problem

An opponent falls out of contact. The race loses its rival and the player loses a reason to push.

Check conditions

  • Player position
  • Camera visibility
  • Player acceleration

If all conditions allow

Reposition the AI so it is relevant again, out of sight.

Otherwise

Leave it driving. No visible jump.

The implementation is tuned around the AI distance and reposition-delay parameters, including MaxAllowedDistance and TeleportationDelay.

Trade-offs I did not hide

RiskPlayer effectShipped design response
Visible poppingBreaks immersion and looks like a bug.Only reposition when the car is off camera.
Perceived unfairnessPlayer feels the AI cheats.Match speed and heading when placing the opponent so its return to the race reads naturally.
Repeated catchesPlayer escapes, gets caught again, feels punished.Per-car cooldown and a cap per race.

The shipped implementation addresses visible popping, perceived unfairness and repeated catch-up by repositioning out of camera view, aligning the car to the track and managing repeated triggers.

Experience overview and goals

A quick-entry combat-racing experience designed to support immediate play, adaptive competition and readable action.

  • Get players into a race in a few steps.
  • Teach controls during play, one action at a time.
  • Keep races engaging for racing veterans and newcomers.
  • Keep AI opponents relevant through adaptive tuning and conditional repositioning.

Core loops

Entry-to-replay loop

    Player opens the game from the platform. Hero screen: one clear play action. Race begins with FTUE prompts in the first race. Result screen communicates win or loss and points based on performance. Replay or return to the platform.
Post-race loop. The result communicates the race outcome and performance-based points, then makes replay the clearest next action.

Moment-to-moment loop

Drive. Steer. Collect rockets, nitros, shields and health. Fire rockets at opponents. Use nitro to gain speed. Use shields to absorb hits. Drawn from the pickup maps, VFX list and sound list.

Audience considerations

Experienced racing players

Need AI that pushes, uses abilities and keeps pace. Risk: the race feels like a tutorial.

New and non-racing players

Need clear prompts, forgiving opponents and a first win within reach. Risk: they quit after being passed and hit repeatedly.

Playtesting feedback informed balancing for both audiences, with the Easy, New Easy and Noob profiles providing distinct tuning targets.

Testing and iteration process

Playtesting was part of the shipped balancing work. I participated in sessions, generated feedback, implemented tweaks and tuned the experience for experienced racing players and players new to the genre.

  1. Observe

    Watch players in a build and capture what they do, where they hesitate and how they use abilities.

  2. Separate needs

    Read feedback through the lens of experienced racers and newcomers.

  3. Target the system

    Adjust AI speed, combat/ability probabilities, or tutorial timing based on the friction.

  4. Re-test

    Play again and iterate until the race feels engaging and understandable for both groups.

Audience-led balancing

AudienceDesign priorityImplemented response
Experienced racersOpponent pressure, meaningful combat choices and competitive pacing.Higher speed and ability-use profiles keep opponents active and create counterplay.
Players new to racing gamesControl clarity, recovery room and a readable introduction to abilities.Easy, New Easy and Noob profiles reduce pressure in different ways; FTUE prompts introduce controls in context.

What the iteration connected

Feedback translated into concrete tuning changes across the first-race experience and AI profile parameters. The Easy and New Easy profiles show differentiated tuning, while the Noob profile supports players who need a gentler entry into the race.

Player difficulty classification

The classifier combines race position, kills, survival time and deaths into a score. That score maps performance into Easy, Medium or Hard and informs the next AI tuning profile.

S = (w1 × P) + (w2 × K) - (w3 × ST) + (w4 × D)
SymbolSource definitionExample weight
PPosition score, 0 to 1. First place = 1, last = 0.w1 = 0.4
KNumber of kills. Higher kills = higher difficulty.w2 = 0.2
STSurvival time percentage. 1 = survived to the end, 0 = died instantly.w3 = 0.3
DDeath factor. Higher deaths reduce difficulty classification.w4 = -0.5

Bands: Easy S < 0.4. Medium 0.4 ≤ S < 0.7. Hard S ≥ 0.7.

AI behaviour and adaptive repositioning

I designed and shipped conditional AI repositioning to keep opponents relevant when they drifted too far from the player.

Signals

  • Player position on the track.
  • Whether the AI is visible to the player camera.
  • Player acceleration.
  • Distance between AI and player. The datasheet sets MaxAllowedDistance = 250.
  • A delay before acting. The datasheet has TeleportationDelay = 0 (Existing) and 0.5 (others).

Intended outcome

Keep opponents within active race context while avoiding obvious camera-visible jumps.

Safety and visibility

  • Never reposition inside the camera view.
  • Place on a valid road position, facing along the track, not overlapping another car.
  • Match the surrounding speed so the car does not appear parked or flying.
  • Skip repositioning for a car that was just hit, so a successful rocket still matters.

Edge cases

  • Player stopped or stuck.
  • Several AI cars flagged at once.
  • The diagonal shortcut, where two valid paths exist.
  • During FTUE prompts.
  • Near the finish on the last lap.

Runtime behaviour

  • Evaluate player position, camera visibility, acceleration and AI distance before repositioning.
  • Place the opponent on a valid road position, aligned to the track and outside the player camera view.
  • Respect recent hits, simultaneous reposition triggers, FTUE prompts and the finish-line context.

The shipped behaviour uses player position, camera visibility, acceleration and AI distance. MaxAllowedDistance = 250, while TeleportationDelay is 0 for Existing and 0.5 for the other tuning profiles.

Delivered systems and design principles

The project brought together fast-entry UX, level planning, adaptive AI, playtest-led balancing, sound design, VFX supervision and cross-discipline art coordination into one cohesive racing experience.

Fast entry

A lock-and-go path and in-race FTUE get players into action quickly and introduce controls when they become useful.

Adaptive opponents

Difficulty profiles tune opponent speed and ability use for different levels of racing experience.

Relevant race pressure

Conditional repositioning addresses AI drift and keeps opponents part of the player’s active race.

Iteration through feedback

User testing and feedback informed balancing changes for experienced racing players and newcomers.

Design principles carried forward

  • Reduce friction between discovering a game and playing it.
  • Keep opponent behaviour engaging without making the race feel unfair.
  • Teach mechanics through action, using prompts at the moment they matter.
  • Turn playtest observations into targeted, implemented changes.
  • Use sound and VFX to communicate speed, impact, pickup collection and ability states.
